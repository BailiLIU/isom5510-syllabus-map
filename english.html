Topic 0 · What Do the Data Tell Us?

Syllabus scope: Describing data & probability — summarize messy data, identify patterns, and describe randomness.

0.1 Describing Data
Data typesCenterSpreadDistributionVisualization
What is the object?

Turn raw observations into a structured description: what variables exist, what scale they use, and what units or categories they represent.

Central tendency

Typical-value summaries such as mean or median answer “where is the data centered?”

Variation

Range, variance, standard deviation, and related summaries answer “how dispersed are observations?”

Shape & pattern

Skewness, clusters, outliers, multimodality, and visual structure matter because two datasets with similar averages can behave very differently.

0.2 Probability & Randomness
Random variable

A numerical representation of an uncertain outcome.

Distribution

A rule describing possible outcomes and how probability is allocated among them.

Expectation & variance

Expected value summarizes long-run center; variance measures dispersion around that center.

Course logic
Probability provides the language needed later for sampling distributions, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests.

Topic 1 · Can We Learn About Many from a Few?

Syllabus scope: Sampling & estimation — choosing a good sample, estimating something that cannot be observed completely, and understanding why estimates change across samples.

1.1 Population, Sample, Parameter, Statistic
ConceptMeaningRole
PopulationThe full group we care about.Target of inference.
SampleThe subset actually observed.Available evidence.
ParameterA numerical property of the population.Usually unknown.
StatisticA numerical summary computed from the sample.Used to estimate the parameter.
1.2 Sampling Design & Representativeness

The key question is not only “how large is the sample?” but also “how was it selected?” A large but systematically biased sample can still give a misleading answer.

  • Simple random sampling: every sample of the specified size is equally likely.
  • Systematic sampling: observations are selected at a regular interval after a starting point.
  • Selection bias / nonresponse: the observed sample may differ systematically from the target population.
  • Reproducibility: when software is used for random sampling, recording the random seed helps reproduce the procedure.
1.3 Point Estimation
Mean

Use the sample mean to estimate the population mean.

Proportion

Use the sample proportion to estimate the population proportion.

Variance

Use sample variance to estimate population variability.

1.4 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

MLE chooses the parameter value that makes the observed data most plausible under the assumed statistical model.

  • Choose a model.
  • Write the likelihood of the observed sample as a function of the unknown parameter.
  • Find the parameter value that maximizes that likelihood.
  • Interpret the maximizing value as a point estimate.
1.5 Sampling Variability, LLN, and CLT
Sampling variability

Different random samples produce different estimates. The estimate itself is therefore random before the sample is observed.

Law of Large Numbers

As sample size grows, averages stabilize around the population mean under suitable conditions.

Central Limit Theorem

For sufficiently large samples, the distribution of many sample averages becomes approximately normal under suitable conditions, making uncertainty quantifiable.

Important distinction
More data reduces random sampling variability, but it does not automatically repair a biased sampling process.

Topic 2 · How Uncertain Is Our Answer?

Syllabus scope: Confidence intervals & bootstrap — assess precision and how much trust to place in a number produced from one dataset.

2.1 Standard Error & Precision

A point estimate gives one number. Standard error describes how much that estimator would vary across repeated samples.

  • Smaller standard error → more precise estimate.
  • Larger sample size usually reduces standard error.
  • Precision is not the same as lack of bias; a very precise estimate can still target the wrong population.
2.2 Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval combines a point estimate with a margin that reflects sampling uncertainty. Its role is to report a range of plausible values for the population parameter under the method's assumptions.

Center

The point estimate.

Width

Driven by standard error and the chosen confidence level.

2.3 Bootstrap

Bootstrap methods approximate the sampling behavior of an estimator by repeatedly resampling from the observed sample. This is especially useful when a direct theoretical sampling distribution is inconvenient or unavailable.

  • Resample from the observed data.
  • Recompute the statistic many times.
  • Use the resulting empirical distribution to assess uncertainty.

Topic 3 · Is the Pattern Real, or Could It Be Chance?

Syllabus scope: Hypothesis testing — determine whether a strategy is actually better and whether an observed difference is statistically meaningful.

3.1 Hypotheses & Evidence
Null hypothesis

A benchmark claim such as “no improvement” or “no difference.”

Alternative hypothesis

The competing claim the analysis is looking for evidence to support.

3.2 Test Statistic & p-value

A test statistic measures how far the observed data depart from the null model. A p-value summarizes how surprising results at least this extreme would be if the null hypothesis were true.

3.3 Statistical Significance vs Practical Importance

A statistically detectable difference is not automatically a meaningful business improvement. The practical size and consequences of an effect still matter.

Topic 4 · How Are Variables Related—and What Can We Predict?

Syllabus scope: Regression — understand factors associated with an outcome, make predictions, and recognize omitted-variable problems.

4.1 Relationship Modeling

Regression expresses how an outcome variable changes with one or more explanatory variables. The central questions are direction, magnitude, uncertainty, and model fit.

4.2 Prediction

Once a relationship is modeled, regression can generate predictions for new cases. Prediction quality depends on whether the model structure and the new data are sufficiently similar to the conditions under which the model was estimated.

4.3 Omitted Variables & Interpretation Risk

If an important variable is omitted and is related to included predictors, the estimated relationships may be distorted. This is one reason association should not automatically be interpreted as causation.
