ISOM5620 · Visual Analysis for Business Decision

ISOM5620 is about visual analysis for business decisions: beginning with the business context, exploring data, identifying a defensible signal, selecting evidence, and communicating a “Big Idea” that can change what a decision-maker understands or considers doing. The Studio 1 materials place the analyst in a management-facing role and explicitly separate a raw finding from a point of view and an actionable Big Idea.

The AACSB assessment for the course emphasizes two capabilities: using modern technology/methods/support systems for advanced analysis, and identifying the best approach, tools, and data sources for a given business problem.

1 · Business Context Before Visualization

Audience → concern → decision

The course treats visualization as a decision-support activity. Before building charts, ask:

  • Who? Who is the primary audience?
  • Care? What does this person care about?
  • Decide? What decision or action might the analysis inform?

This prevents “chart-first” analysis and forces the analyst to define why the analysis exists.

2 · Exploratory Visual Analysis

Dimensions, measures, and comparisons

Dimensions

Order Date, Region, Category, Sub-Category and similar grouping fields define how the business is sliced.

Measures

Sales, Profit, margin, discount, and other numerical outcomes quantify performance.

Exploration asks questions such as: how performance changes over time; where sales and profit come from; which categories/regions/products diverge; whether weak margins coincide with discounting or persist over time.

3 · Observation → Evidence → Insight

Find the signal

A useful insight is more than an unusual number. The Studio framework is:

  1. Observation: what did you find in exploratory analysis?
  2. Evidence / filter: what data supports the finding?
  3. Signal: why should the decision-maker care?
The Studio defines an insight as a meaningful interpretation that changes what someone understands or considers doing.

4 · Chart Choice, Attention, and Evidence

The plot must defend the claim
  • Does the chart directly support the Big Idea?
  • Is it the strongest evidence available?
  • What is still missing or unclear?
  • Can the viewer quickly see the comparison or pattern that matters?

The course schedule and Studio materials point toward later work on chart choice, clutter, directing attention, and visual design.

5 · Big Idea & Data Storytelling

Finding → Point of View → Big Idea

Finding states what happened. Point of view interprets why the finding matters. Big Idea connects that interpretation to what is at stake and what management should investigate or do next.

Example structure: “Strong sales in X are not translating into profitability; this may be masking a profitability problem that should be investigated before pursuing more growth.”

6 · Tool Selection & Professional Judgment

AACSB learning objectives
  • Use state-of-the-art technology, methods, and support systems to conduct advanced analyses.
  • Identify the best approach, tools, and data sources for a given business problem.

This means mastering Tableau is not enough: the analyst must also justify why the chosen data, approach, and visual evidence are appropriate for the decision.
